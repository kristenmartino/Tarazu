Opening sentence
“Tarazu keeps the evidence, assumptions and reasoning behind a product decision easy to revisit. Here is a fictional team moving from a reminder hypothesis to a smaller readiness experiment before committing to a build.”

1. Home → Start decision walkthrough → inspect the scoring basis (one minute)
The reminder edit form is the first stop. Open the Strategy Brief separately if useful: the shared period is a 30-day pilot, the audience is 100 users, and the assumed baseline is 30% of approvals overdue by more than 48 hours. Capacity is one engineer for two weeks; notification fatigue is a proposed guardrail.
Reach 55 is a band score, not 55 people. Its selected existing claim forecasts 60 of 100 users in 30 days. Impact 55 maps to a forecast reduction from 30% to 25% overdue: 5 percentage points, not 5 percent. Effort 35 maps to an estimate of 3 total person-days for the scoped in-app pilot, not elapsed duration. Confidence 20 is the Assumed evidence band, not a 20% success probability. Expand a recorded claim to read its full caveats and linked sources. Selection explains a score; it does not verify the claim or recalculate it.
Cancel the form when done; avoid accepting new AI values during the prepared walkthrough.

2. Priorities: compare next learning steps (one minute)
Current calibrated inputs rank the request-readiness checklist at 7,500, reminders at 1,729 and the status dashboard at 191. These are relative indices, not benefit ratios or ROI. Each candidate is scoped differently: one product/design day for a paper checklist exercise, three total person-days for a reminder pilot, and ten for a read-only dashboard pilot. Compare the next learning step, not three equivalent production builds. More work and engineering review would be needed for production.
The original saved decision snapshots contain earlier estimates. Do not describe those frozen rankings as the current list or claim that evidence alone mathematically caused the reranking; the scoring rubric, scope and judgments were clarified too.

3. Signals: inspect support and conflict (one minute)
The administrator exercise suggests overlooked requests and manual chasing; the approver exercise suggests missing information is another cause. Requester and dashboard notes support competing options. The small fictional samples overlap or differ: their counts are not independent votes.
Show “Round B: four of five cases were incomplete”. Those five invented cases challenge a reminder-only approach without proving checklist effectiveness, population prevalence or causal benefit.

4. Validate: reopen the saved investigation (one minute)
Continue the latest saved brief for “Approval reminders — synthetic”. Revision 5 includes the added Signal, changed assumption, risks and next action. Four of seven areas have illustrative ratings; market pull, differentiation and monetization remain unassessed. The investigation is incomplete. Evidence attachments require explicit human review. Saving a brief does not approve a decision; changed sources or context can require reviewing evidence again.

The guest example includes only the two story decisions.
“Round A: investigate reminder versus readiness” preserves brief revision 4 and its original two attachments.

Both decisions are drafts, not approved builds or observed outcomes.
